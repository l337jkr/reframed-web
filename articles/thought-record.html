How to Write a CBT Thought Record

The core exercise of cognitive behavioural therapy, in five columns — with a worked example from a bad Tuesday.

A thought record is the exercise a CBT therapist will hand you in roughly week two. It looks like homework and it is, but the mechanism is worth understanding: writing the thought down moves it from something you're inside of to something you're looking at.

Classic versions have seven columns. Five is enough to get the effect, and five is short enough that you'll actually finish one at 11pm.

Column 1 — The situation

What happened, in the register of a security camera. Who, what, where, when. No interpretation, no adjectives that do the judging for you.

Write"2:40pm, team standup. I gave the update on the migration. Priya asked when the timeline slipped."

Not "I got grilled in standup." That's already the distorted version, and if you start there the rest of the record just decorates it.

Column 2 — The automatic thought

The sentence that ran through your head. Not a tidy summary of it — the actual words, including the ugly ones. Then rate how much you believe it, 0 to 100.

Write"They've worked out I'm not up to this job." Belief: 85

The belief rating matters more than it looks. It's the before-measurement, and without it you can't tell at the end whether anything moved.

If nothing comes, work backwards from the feeling: what would I have to be thinking to feel this? The thought is usually sitting right underneath, unexamined because it never had to defend itself.

Column 3 — The emotion

One word, plus an intensity from 0 to 100. Shame. Anxiety. Anger. Humiliation.

Write"Shame — 75. Anxiety — 60."

One word, not a paragraph. "I felt like everyone could see through me" is another thought wearing a feeling's coat; put it in column two where it belongs. The discipline of a single word is what makes the rating meaningful.

Column 4 — The distortion

Name the shape. Mind reading, in the example above — you've decided what the room concluded about you without anyone saying it. Often two or three overlap; write them all.

Write"Mind reading. Labelling ('not up to this job'). Mental filter — one question, ignoring the rest of the standup."

Column 5 — The balanced thought

This is the column people get wrong, so it's worth being precise about the target. You are not writing the opposite of the automatic thought. You are writing what a fair witness — someone with all the information and no stake in your humiliation — would have said.

Three questions get you there:

  • What's the evidence for the thought? Not feelings. Things that would hold up if someone else reviewed them.
  • What's the evidence against it? Usually the harder list, because the mental filter has been discarding it all day.
  • What would I say to a friend who told me this? The answer is nearly always kinder and more accurate at once, which tells you something.
Write"Priya asked one factual question about a date. She asks everyone factual questions — that's her job. Two people said the migration plan was clearer than the last one. I don't have evidence about what anyone concluded about me, only about what was asked." Belief in original: 30. Shame: 35.

Then re-rate. If the belief in the original thought dropped, the record worked. If it didn't move at all, the balanced thought is probably a slogan rather than an argument — go back and get more specific about the evidence.

Four mistakes that make a thought record useless

1. Writing the interpretation in column one

"I got humiliated in standup" contaminates every column after it. Start with the camera footage.

2. Sanitising the automatic thought

If the thought was "I'm pathetic," write "I'm pathetic." The polite paraphrase is a different thought, and the one you actually believe goes unchallenged.

3. Reaching for positivity instead of accuracy

"I'm great at my job, actually" is a claim your brain can immediately refute, and it will. "One factual question is not a verdict" is small, defensible, and survives contact.

4. Only doing it when things are bad

Records written during a spike are the hardest and least accurate. A short one on an ordinary day builds the skill you'll need on the bad one.

How often, and for how long

Two or three a week is plenty, and most take under five minutes once the format is familiar. The value compounds in a way a single record doesn't show: after a month you can look back and see that the same three distortions produce most of your bad afternoons. That's actionable in a way "I've been stressed lately" never is.

The goal isn't to keep writing records forever. It's to run the loop — trigger, emotion, distortion, reframe — quickly enough in your head that you stop needing the paper.

A note on limits

Thought records are a self-help tool with good evidence behind them as part of CBT, but they aren't treatment on their own, and they aren't the right first move for trauma or crisis. If your mood is severe, persistent, or worsening, work with a professional. If you're in crisis, contact your local emergency service or a crisis line.
