Cognitive Biases vs Cognitive Distortions: What's the Difference?

Two lists of thinking errors from two research traditions. Which ones matter for your own thoughts, and where to start.

In short

Cognitive biases are systematic errors in judgment studied in decision-making research (Kahneman and Tversky). Cognitive distortions are exaggerated thinking patterns that fuel painful emotions, catalogued in CBT (Beck). The lists overlap. For everyday life, start with negativity bias, confirmation bias, catastrophising, mind reading, all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralisation and social comparison.

The short answer

A cognitive bias is a systematic error in judgment that affects everyone, like anchoring on the first price you see. A cognitive distortion is an exaggerated pattern of thinking, like assuming the worst, that tends to fuel painful emotions. Biases come from judgment and decision-making research. Distortions come from cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). The two lists overlap, but they aren't the same list.

Two traditions, two purposes

Biases were mapped by researchers like Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky from the 1970s onward. They asked how people make judgments under uncertainty and found predictable errors: anchoring, availability, overconfidence. The point was to describe how minds work, not to treat anyone.

Distortions were named by Aaron Beck and later clinicians working with depression and anxiety. They noticed that distressed people's thoughts followed recognisable shapes, such as catastrophising, mind reading and all-or-nothing thinking, and that those shapes could be caught and tested. The APA describes cognitive therapy as helping people identify problematic thoughts and weigh them against evidence. Distortions are the labels that make that work possible.

So a bias can be socially important, driving polarisation or bad investments, without being the most useful thing to work on in your own head at 11pm. The 12 biases shaping 2026 are ranked for their effect on society. The list below is ranked for usefulness in everyday thoughts.

Catastrophising, mind reading, all-or-nothing thinking and overgeneralisation aren't on the societal list at all. That's deliberate. They're among the most commonly taught distortions in CBT because they show up in the thoughts behind a bad day far more often than anchoring does.

Where the lists overlap

Some biases do the same work as a distortion:

  • Confirmation bias and mental filter. Once you believe you're unlikable, you notice every cold reply and forget the warm ones.
  • Negativity bias and discounting the positive. One insult outweighs a hundred kind comments, and the kind ones "don't count".
  • Availability bias and overgeneralisation. A few vivid examples become "this always happens".
  • Fundamental attribution error and labelling. One late reply becomes "they're rude", a verdict on a person, not a reading of a situation.

These are the biases worth practising on personally: they feed straight into how you feel about yourself and other people.

Learn the pattern before the name

You don't need the vocabulary to use the method. "Am I assuming the worst?" is easier to answer in the moment than "Is this catastrophising?" The terms are useful later, once you've recognised the pattern in your own thoughts a few times and want a short handle for it. The aim is to notice the pattern while it's running, not to memorise a glossary.

Questions people ask

Is confirmation bias a cognitive distortion?

Not formally, since it comes from decision-making research. But it works like the CBT distortion called mental filter, which is why it's worth catching in your own thoughts.

Are cognitive distortions a sign of a mental health condition?

No. Everyone uses them. They become a problem when they're frequent, rigid and drive distress, which is when CBT, self-guided or with a therapist, can help.

What does CBT do about them?

It helps you identify the thought, test it against evidence, and replace it with a more accurate one. The target is accuracy, not positivity. The thought record is the standard way to do it.

A note on limits

This is general education drawn from CBT, not a diagnosis or treatment. If your thoughts are tied to persistent low mood, anxiety or distress, or are getting worse, a mental health professional can help. If you're in crisis, contact your local emergency service or a crisis line.
