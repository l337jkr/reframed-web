# Why Do You Keep Repeating the Same Patterns Even When You Know Better?

> Understand the psychology behind self-sabotage, avoidance and recurring behaviour, and learn practical CBT techniques for breaking unhealthy patterns.

> **In short:** Recurring behavioural patterns are rarely a simple failure of discipline. Behaviours like procrastination, perfectionism, people-pleasing and emotional withdrawal persist because they deliver an immediate short-term reward — relief from anxiety, criticism or vulnerability — at the expense of long-term progress (Beck, 2020). Breaking the cycle means spotting your trigger, naming the belief and short-term payoff, and testing a manageable alternative response.

You promised yourself that this time would be different.

You wouldn't overthink the relationship. You wouldn't procrastinate on your goals. You wouldn't keep seeking approval from people who make you question your worth.

But eventually, you found yourself repeating the same behaviour.

It's frustrating, especially when you're aware of what you're doing.

However, recurring behavioural patterns aren't always a simple failure of discipline. Sometimes, they persist because they serve a short-term psychological function.

## Self-sabotage can be a form of self-protection

Imagine someone who wants to start a business but continually postpones launching their product.

They spend months researching, planning and perfecting every detail.

From the outside, it looks like procrastination.

But underneath that behaviour might be a belief:

> **Protective belief:** "If I launch something and it fails, I'll discover that I'm not as capable as I thought."

Avoiding the launch protects them from confronting that possibility.

In the short term, avoidance reduces anxiety.

In the long term, it prevents them from gaining experience, receiving feedback and discovering what they're actually capable of.

The behaviour provides immediate emotional protection at the expense of future progress (for the research history on self-handicapping and mood repair, see [what CBT says about self-sabotage](https://behaviorized.net/articles/stop-self-sabotage.html)).

## The hidden reward behind unhealthy patterns

Consider these common examples:

*Six recurring self-sabotaging behaviours compared by their short-term psychological reward and long-term cost*

| Behaviour | Possible short-term reward | Potential long-term cost |
|---|---|---|
| People-pleasing | Avoiding conflict | Resentment and neglected needs |
| Perfectionism | Reducing exposure to criticism | Delayed progress |
| Overthinking | Feeling prepared or in control | Indecision and exhaustion |
| Emotional withdrawal | Avoiding vulnerability | Difficulty developing intimacy |
| Seeking reassurance | Temporary reduction in anxiety | Greater reliance on external certainty |
| Procrastination | Escaping immediate discomfort | Stress and missed opportunities |

These explanations won't apply to everyone. The same behaviour can serve different functions depending on the person and their circumstances.

The important question is what the behaviour does for you in the moment.

## Understanding the cycle

CBT provides a framework for examining how thoughts, feelings and behaviours interact (Beck, 2020; Healthdirect Australia).

Consider a perfectionism-avoidance cycle:

- **Trigger:** An important opportunity appears.
- **Thought:** If I don't perform exceptionally well, I'll embarrass myself.
- **Emotion:** Anxiety and pressure.
- **Behaviour:** Delay taking action.
- **Short-term consequence:** Relief from anxiety.
- **Long-term consequence:** Guilt, missed opportunities and reduced confidence.

The next time an opportunity appears, the cycle may repeat.

Avoidance can reinforce itself because it temporarily removes an uncomfortable emotional experience.

Breaking the pattern requires finding a different response to that discomfort.

## How to start breaking a recurring pattern

### 1. Identify your trigger

Notice the situations in which the behaviour tends to emerge.

Is it criticism? Uncertainty? Rejection? Feeling misunderstood? The possibility of failure?

### 2. Identify the belief underneath it

Ask yourself what you're predicting will happen if you respond differently.

What are you afraid of discovering or experiencing?

### 3. Recognise the short-term reward

What discomfort does your existing behaviour help you escape?

Understanding this can help explain why the pattern persists, even when you're aware of its consequences.

### 4. Choose a manageable alternative

Don't expect yourself to eliminate years of habitual behaviour in a single decision.

If you tend to avoid tasks because they feel overwhelming, start with ten minutes.

If you struggle with boundaries, practise expressing a small preference.

If you overanalyse decisions, set a reasonable time limit for one low-stakes choice.

### 5. Observe what actually happens

Compare your prediction with the outcome.

Was the situation as threatening as you expected? Were you able to tolerate the discomfort? What did you learn?

This is where reflection becomes a tool for learning rather than another opportunity for self-criticism (see [how to run a two-minute CBT check on your phone right before you stall](https://behaviorized.net/articles/stop-self-sabotage-with-cbt-apps.html)).

## Discomfort doesn't always mean you're doing something wrong

One of the challenges of changing an established pattern is that a healthier response may initially feel unfamiliar.

Setting a boundary can feel selfish.

Submitting imperfect work can feel irresponsible.

Being emotionally honest can feel dangerous.

Taking action without complete certainty can feel reckless.

Context matters, and discomfort should not automatically be interpreted as something to push through. But unfamiliarity alone doesn't establish that a behaviour is wrong.

Sometimes, change requires learning to tolerate the emotions that your old patterns helped you avoid.

## Build a different response, one experience at a time

You don't break a behavioural pattern by hating yourself for having it.

You break it by understanding what maintains it, developing alternative responses and practising those responses repeatedly.

## Questions people ask

### Why does self-criticism rarely stop a bad habit?

Because self-sabotage is usually driven by threat and discomfort in the first place. Punishing yourself increases shame and anxiety, making the short-term relief of avoidance even more tempting next time.

### How do I tell whether discomfort is a warning sign or just unfamiliarity?

Check the objective facts of the situation rather than the intensity of the feeling alone. If your safety and values are intact — for example, declining an extra favour politely or sending a checked draft — the discomfort is usually the feeling your old habit used to hide, not proof of danger.

### How small can an alternative behaviour be and still count?

Small enough that you will genuinely complete it today: ten minutes on an avoided project, one honest sentence in a conversation, or waiting fifteen minutes before seeking reassurance. Consistency updates beliefs faster than occasional heroic effort.

## A note on limits

This article is general educational information inspired by CBT principles, not clinical therapy or medical advice. Recurring avoidance, emotional withdrawal or difficulty starting tasks can also be connected to clinical anxiety, depression, trauma or ADHD. If these patterns cause significant distress or impairment, speak with a qualified mental health professional, and if you are in crisis, contact your local emergency service or a crisis helpline immediately.
