CBT Tools Compared: How to Choose a CBT App (and Why Reframed Is Built for Mindset)

Paper workbooks, anxiety toolkits, digital thought diaries, AI chatbots, and Reframed. What each format does well, where each falls short, and how to pick the right one for the job.

In short

"CBT app" covers at least four different products: clinical anxiety toolkits (MindShift CBT), multi-feature thought diaries (Clarity), conversational AI chatbots (Wysa, after Woebot retired its consumer app in June 2025), and mindset-focused restructuring companions (Reframed), alongside paper workbooks. Across 176 randomised trials, apps that teach active CBT skills outperform passive mood logging (Linardon et al., 2024). If your goal is acute panic relief or Android support, other tools fit better. If your goal is changing a recurring mindset or self-image on iOS, Reframed is built specifically for that job: short daily reps, personality-matched prompts, protocol-bound AI, no streaks to break, and local on-device storage. Disclosure: this guide is published by Hexp Studios, maker of Reframed.

Search an app store for "CBT" and you get meditation timers, mood trackers, gratitude journals, AI companions, and clinical worksheets all wearing the same three letters. Most of them are fine at what they actually are. Very few do the same thing.

Choosing well starts with separating the goal. Calming down during a panic spike, venting at 1am, completing exposure homework for a phobia, and changing a long-running belief like "I never follow through" are four different jobs. This guide compares the main categories of CBT tools available in 2026, lays out what the clinical research says about smartphone CBT, and explains why we built Reframed specifically for mindset and self-image work, including the cases where you should pick something else.

What the research says about CBT apps

The largest synthesis to date is a 2024 meta-analysis in World Psychiatry by Jake Linardon and colleagues, pooling 176 randomised controlled trials of mental health smartphone apps. Across trials, apps produced small-to-moderate, statistically reliable reductions in depressive symptoms (g = 0.28) and generalised anxiety symptoms (g = 0.26) compared with control conditions, building on an earlier 66-trial review that also found reductions in stress (Linardon et al., 2019; 2024).

Two details in those reviews matter when you're comparing tools:

  • Active CBT beats passive tracking. Effect sizes were consistently larger when apps included explicit cognitive behavioural exercises, such as examining and restructuring unhelpful thoughts, rather than simply logging a mood score from one to five. Recording that Tuesday felt bad doesn't change why Wednesday will feel the same.
  • Consistency beats session length. Self-guided tools fail mostly through drop-off. Heavy, multi-screen exercises get abandoned once motivation dips; brief, repeatable exercises build the habit that produces cognitive change over weeks (Lally et al., 2010).

1. Paper workbooks and public clinical modules

Books like Greenberger and Padesky's Mind Over Mood and the free PDF workbooks from Western Australia's Centre for Clinical Interventions (CCI) remain the clinical gold standard for depth. They walk through core beliefs, conditional rules, and behavioural experiments without notifications or subscriptions. Their weakness is timing: an automatic thought usually hits in a hallway, on a commute, or at midnight in bed, where you rarely have a workbook and a pen.

2. Clinical anxiety and exposure toolkits (MindShift CBT)

Developed by the nonprofit Anxiety Canada, MindShift CBT is one of the strongest free mental health apps on either platform. Because it includes fear ladders (breaking an avoided situation into ranked steps) and quick panic coping cards, it suits acute anxiety, social anxiety, and phobias better than general mindset apps do. It is less aimed at people whose main problem is perfectionism, self-image, or subtle everyday self-sabotage rather than clinical worry.

3. All-in-one digital thought diaries (Clarity and classic diary apps)

Apps like Clarity translate CBT worksheets, mood tracking, and educational crash courses into a polished mobile hub. They work well if you want mood graphs, guided journaling topics, and audio lessons in one place across iOS and Android. The trade-off for mindset change is breadth: when a tool mixes free-form journaling, mood scores, and lessons, many users default to venting about their day rather than testing a specific belief against checkable evidence.

4. Conversational AI chatbots (Wysa and chat-first tools)

With Woebot's consumer shutdown in 2025, Wysa is the best-known CBT-informed chatbot still widely available to individuals. A conversational interface lowers the barrier when you feel overwhelmed and want something to guide you line by line. For long-term mindset work, however, open-ended or chat-heavy tools carry a specific risk: outsourced reassurance. If an AI talks you down every evening, you feel better tonight, lean on the bot's fluency, and still haven't trained your own brain to catch the distortion unaided.

Why changing a mindset is a different job

"Mindset" gets used loosely online to mean motivation or optimism. In CBT terms, a mindset is an organised set of core beliefs ("I don't finish things", "I'm behind everyone else") and rules ("If I can't do it properly, don't start") that filter how you interpret neutral events (Beck, 2020; see Psycho-Cybernetics and CBT).

Three popular app mechanics struggle to move that layer:

  • Mood tracking logs the weather, not the lens. Knowing you felt anxious four days out of seven doesn't expose the mind-reading thought that produced all four days.
  • Blank-page journaling rehearses the story. Writing three paragraphs about how badly a meeting went often deepens rumination unless something interrupts the interpretation and asks for camera-level evidence.
  • Daily affirmations trigger counter-arguments. As Wood, Perunovic and Lee (2009) showed, repeating an upbeat trait claim you don't yet believe ("I am confident and disciplined") prompts your memory to retrieve all the times you weren't. See why affirmations backfire and reframing works.

To shift a mindset, a tool has to do something narrower and more forensic: catch the exact sentence the old belief just produced, name the distortion in it, test it against facts a fair witness would accept, and store that verdict so the case against the old identity accumulates over time.

Why Reframed is built the way it is

Reframed was designed specifically for that forensic loop, particularly for people who are already self-aware enough to describe their patterns ("I know I catastrophise") yet still run them under stress. Five design choices separate it from general wellness apps and chatbots:

1. Mindset change by verdict, not repetition

Reframed treats an old self-belief like "I always blow opportunities" as a testable claim rather than a mood to soothe. Each time you run a five-step thought record, you write a balanced reframe grounded in specific, checkable facts, and re-rate how much you believe the original thought. Over weeks, those entries form a written file of counter-evidence. Affirmation cards exist in the app, matched to your personality, but they sit at the end of the method to consolidate a belief you've already proved, never at the start as wishful thinking.

2. Personality-matched daily prompts instead of generic check-ins

A perfectionist who over-prepares and an avoidant thinker who ghosts under pressure don't run the same distortions, and they don't respond to the same questions. During onboarding, Reframed pairs your focus areas (such as perfectionism, anxiety, relationships, or self-worth) with a short Big Five assessment mapped to an MBTI-style cognitive function stack. Instead of "How was your day?", you get a two-minute daily prompt aimed at the specific thinking trap you lean on:

Generic app prompt"How are you feeling today, and what are three things you're grateful for?" Targeted mindset prompt"What standard did you miss today? Write it out, then write who set it."

3. Protocol-bound AI, not an open-ended companion

When you capture an automatic thought in Reframed, the AI doesn't try to be a friend, an oracle, or an endless chat thread. It runs one clinical CBT protocol: trigger, emotion, cognitive distortion in your own words, and balanced reframe. AI requests are also capped at a fair-use limit of roughly ten reframed thoughts per day. That keeps pricing predictable (including a one-time lifetime option) and reinforces a core CBT principle: mindset changes through a couple of honest daily reps, not a forty-minute midnight conversation with a bot.

4. No streaks to protect, no feed to scroll

Gamified streaks help with language flashcards and backfire badly in mental health tools. For anyone prone to all-or-nothing thinking, losing a 19-day streak triggers the exact distortion the app was meant to treat ("I ruined the run, why bother"). Reframed tracks your distortion frequency, belief shifts, and emotion patterns in its Insights tab without punishing you for having a human week.

5. Local on-device storage

Thought records fail the moment you sanitise your own thoughts (see Mistake 2 in our thought record guide). If the real thought was petty, ashamed, or ugly, and part of you wonders where a cloud account stores it, you will write a polite paraphrase and challenge the wrong belief. Reframed stores your reflections, thought records, and personality profile locally on your device, with no user account to create and no behavioural analytics SDK watching what you tap.

When to choose a different tool

No single CBT tool is best for every person or problem. Pick something else in any of these cases:

  • You use Android, or want a completely free tool: start with MindShift CBT or the free PDF workbooks from the Centre for Clinical Interventions.
  • Your main goal is overcoming a specific phobia or panic attacks: exposure hierarchies ("fear ladders") and interoceptive exercises matter more than belief journaling here; MindShift CBT or CBT with a clinician is better suited.
  • You want conversational back-and-forth or human text coaching: look at Wysa rather than a structured thought-record tool.
  • Your symptoms are severe, persistent, or tied to trauma, OCD, or major depression: self-guided apps have modest average effect sizes (Linardon et al., 2024) and are not a substitute for an evidence-based therapist. Use an app alongside sessions if your clinician agrees, not instead of care.

Five questions to test any CBT app in ten minutes

Whichever app you download, run one real thought from your week through it and check five things:

  1. Does it separate the situation from the story? If it lets you log "I got humiliated in standup" as a fact without asking what a camera would have recorded, it won't catch distortions cleanly.
  2. Does it aim for accuracy rather than cheerfulness? A good tool helps you write a defensible sentence you actually believe, not a pep talk.
  3. Will you still open it on an ordinary Wednesday? Two minutes is sustainable; twenty fields of mandatory sliders usually isn't.
  4. What happens if you miss three days? Look for calm continuity, not guilt notifications or broken-streak badges.
  5. Can you read its privacy policy in two minutes? Check whether entries live on your phone or on a company server, and whether behavioural analytics SDKs are bundled.

Questions people ask

Do CBT apps actually work?

Yes, with realistic expectations. Across 176 randomised controlled trials, mental health apps showed small-to-moderate, statistically significant reductions in depression and anxiety symptoms, with stronger results when apps actively used CBT techniques rather than passive tracking (Linardon et al., 2024). They work best as a daily skill practice or a companion to therapy.

Is Reframed a replacement for therapy?

No. Reframed is a self-guided daily practice built on CBT techniques, not therapy and not a medical device. Many people use a thought-record app between therapy sessions so they don't lose the thread during the other 167 hours of the week.

What happened to Woebot?

Woebot Health shut down its direct-to-consumer mobile app on 30 June 2025 to focus on enterprise and healthcare system deployments. Existing consumer comparisons that still list Woebot as a free app-store download are out of date.

Can I use Reframed without AI features?

Yes. Logging thoughts, recording evidence, tracking beliefs, and reviewing your history on-device do not require AI and have no daily cap. When you do use AI features to spot distortions or draft reframes, they follow a structured CBT protocol within a daily fair-use limit.
