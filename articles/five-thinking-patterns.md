# Five Thinking Patterns to Catch First

> Catastrophising, mind reading, all-or-nothing thinking, confirmation bias and overgeneralisation: the five thinking patterns worth catching first, each with a plain question that tests it and a worked example.

> **In short:** Five thinking patterns drive a large share of everyday distress: catastrophising, mind reading, all-or-nothing thinking, confirmation bias and overgeneralisation. Each can be tested with one plain question, such as "Are you assuming the worst?" Learn to spot the pattern first; the psychological term can come later.

There are dozens of named cognitive biases and distortions. You don't need them all. These five are common, they drive a large share of everyday distress, and each one can be tested with a single plain question you can ask yourself in the moment.

Each pattern below starts with the question you'd actually ask, not the term. "Are you assuming the worst?" is easier to answer at 11pm than "Is this catastrophising?" The names are there for later, once you've spotted the pattern in your own head a few times.

## 1. Are you assuming the worst?

**The pattern:** catastrophising, treating a setback as proof that everything will fail.

> **Thought:** "I missed the deadline. I'm going to lose this job."

**The question:** What's the worst outcome you're predicting, and how likely is it?

> **Reframe:** "Worst case is a hard conversation with my manager. Losing the job over one late deliverable is unlikely, and I've hit every other deadline this quarter."

Making the prediction specific is most of the work. A vague dread can't be tested; "I'll be fired by Friday" can.

## 2. Do you actually know what they think?

**The pattern:** mind reading, assuming someone is judging you without them saying so.

> **Thought:** "She went quiet after my joke. She thinks I'm an idiot."

**The question:** What evidence do you have about what this person thinks?

> **Reframe:** "All I saw was a pause. She could have been distracted, tired, or thinking about something else. I don't know, and I could ask."

## 3. Is there anything between perfect and failure?

**The pattern:** all-or-nothing thinking, believing anything short of perfect counts as failure.

> **Thought:** "I skipped the gym twice this week. I've blown it."

**The question:** Is there a possibility between complete success and complete failure?

> **Reframe:** "Three sessions out of five is most of a week. It's more than I managed last month."

## 4. What are you leaving out?

**The pattern:** confirmation bias, collecting proof for what you already believe and dropping the rest.

> **Thought:** "Nobody replied to my message in the group chat. Nobody here likes me."

**The question:** What evidence might you be overlooking?

> **Reframe:** "Two of them invited me out last week, and the chat's been quiet all day for everyone."

This one comes from decision-making research rather than CBT, but in personal life it behaves like a distortion: it keeps a painful belief alive by filtering what you notice. More on that in [cognitive biases vs cognitive distortions](https://behaviorized.net/articles/cognitive-biases-vs-distortions.html).

## 5. Is this one event, or really a pattern?

**The pattern:** overgeneralisation, turning one experience into a rule about your life.

> **Thought:** "I got rejected for this role. Nobody's ever going to hire me."

**The question:** Does this one experience establish a pattern?

> **Reframe:** "One rejection from one company. I've been hired before, and I've applied to four places so far, not forty."

Watch for the words "always", "never", "nobody" and "everyone". They're usually the sign that one data point has become a law.

## How to practise

1. **Catch the thought.** Write it down in the words it used, as soon as you notice a shift in mood.
2. **Ask the question.** Pick whichever of the five fits. Often two do.
3. **Write what the evidence supports.** Not the opposite of the thought, and not a pep talk. The version a fair witness would agree with.

The aim isn't to memorise terms. It's to recognise the patterns working in your own thoughts quickly enough to question them. ## Questions people ask

### Why these five?

They're common, they feed straight into everyday distress, and each can be tested with one question. Four are among the distortions most often taught in CBT; confirmation bias is the decision-making bias that does the most similar work.

### Do I need to know the psychological terms?

No. The plain question works better in the moment. Terms become a useful shorthand later.

### What if the thought is true?

Sometimes it partly is. The questions aren't meant to argue you out of reality. They separate what happened from the story layered on top, so you can deal with the part that's real.

## A note on limits

These questions are self-help drawn from CBT, not a treatment on their own. If your thoughts are tied to persistent low mood, anxiety or distress, or are getting worse, work with a mental health professional. If you're in crisis, contact your local emergency service or a crisis line.
