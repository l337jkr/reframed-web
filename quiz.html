About this quiz

Twenty questions. Ten are uncomfortable situations, like saying how you feel, saying no or being criticised, with four things you might do. Ten are pairs of opposing statements with a five-point scale between them. Every answer counts towards one or more of ten archetypes: familiar ways people protect themselves from failing, being rejected or feeling something uncomfortable. Your result is the one your answers point at most, shown with how it looks from outside, what's really going on underneath, and the one thing that type needs to hear. You also get a code: enter it in the Reframed app and you won't be asked to do the quiz again.

See all ten archetypes

The Optimise-Or-Die Perfectionist

Colour-coded templates, 5am ice baths, a beautiful desk, and a new productivity system every month.

The brutal truth: It's paralysis dressed up as ambition. Researching the best system is how you put off starting, because starting is where you could fail. It's all or nothing with you, so one missed morning writes off the whole week.

What you need to hear: A messy version you finish beats a perfect one you never start. Do it badly today, on purpose, and see what actually happens.

Where to start: Perfectionism

The Anxious Golden Retriever

Three exclamation marks in every text, a reply within thirty seconds, and the loudest supporter of everyone you know.

The brutal truth: You read a full stop at the end of "Okay." as a declaration of war. You say yes while the resentment quietly builds, because a boundary feels like the thing that will make people leave.

What you need to hear: People who only stay while you agree with them aren't staying for you. Say one honest no this week and see who's still there.

Where to start: Avoiding conflict

The Self-Diagnosing Essayist

Fluent in therapy terms, three-hour psychology podcasts, and four-minute voice notes explaining exactly why you're sad.

The brutal truth: You've mistaken going over it again for dealing with it. Turning a feeling into a research paper keeps it at arm's length, so you never have to actually feel it.

What you need to hear: Understanding the cage isn't the same as opening the door. You don't need one more explanation. You need to do one small thing differently.

Where to start: Procrastination

The Nonchalant Final Boss

Lowercase texts, one-word replies fourteen hours later, and a face that says caring about anything is embarrassing.

The brutal truth: Your biggest fear is being seen trying. So you starve every relationship and ambition of effort before it can embarrass you. One foot out the door keeps you in control, and it keeps anyone from getting close.

What you need to hear: Not trying doesn't protect you from failing. It's failing with extra steps. Care about one thing out loud, where someone can see.

Where to start: Fear of failure

The Hyper-Vigilant Checker

A folder of screenshots, symptoms searched at midnight, and every sent message reread five times to check it didn't sound weird.

The brutal truth: You want to be completely sure before you can relax, and the internet will let you check forever. Reassurance works like salt water: every sip makes the next thirst worse.

What you need to hear: You'll never be sure enough, and you don't need to be. Leave one thing unchecked today and notice that you cope.

Where to start: Need for certainty

The Omniscient Micro-Manager

The locked holiday spreadsheet, everyone's live location "for safety", and the restaurant menu read three days ahead.

The brutal truth: You call it being organised. It's worry: anything you can't predict feels like a threat, so you try to bully the future into behaving. You're exhausted because you don't trust anyone else with the smallest detail.

What you need to hear: Most things go fine without you steering them. Hand one thing over this week, don't check on it, and see what happens.

Where to start: Need for certainty

The Autopilot Zombie

A podcast at double speed, a feed in your hand, a comfort show in the background, and "wait, how is it already November?"

The brutal truth: The noise is anaesthetic. The second boredom or an uncomfortable thought turns up, you drown it out until you're numb, and your own life goes past like something on in the background.

What you need to hear: The thought you keep drowning out will still be there when the battery dies. Give it ten quiet minutes and hear what it wants.

Where to start: Procrastination

The Performative Stoic

A minimalist profile, one post every eight months, "protecting my peace", and proud of needing nobody.

The brutal truth: It's avoidance sold as enlightenment. You cut people off at the first sign of friction and use therapy words to dodge ordinary accountability, so you get to leave before anyone can reject you.

What you need to hear: Peace that only lasts while nobody's close isn't peace. It's hiding. Stay in one awkward conversation instead of disappearing.

Where to start: Asking for help

The Main Character Martyr

Posts about how healing isn't linear, cryptic quotes, and a life that plays like an indie film.

The brutal truth: Somewhere along the way you decided that suffering makes you deep, so calm feels boring. When things settle, you quietly unsettle them, because a hard chapter gives you something to tell.

What you need to hear: You're allowed to be fine. You're still interesting without a crisis. Let one good week stay good.

Where to start: Self-criticism

The Cynical Doomer

Dry memes, sarcasm for every occasion, and "it's cooked" as the answer to anything anyone tries.

The brutal truth: Cynicism is the cheapest armour there is. If the whole game is rigged and stupid, you never have to play, and you never have to find out what happens when you try your hardest and it still doesn't work.

What you need to hear: Mocking the people who try doesn't put you above them. It means they're playing and you're watching. Try at one thing, sincerely.

Where to start: Fear of failure

This quiz is for fun and self-reflection, not a diagnosis. Habits like these often run on the same thoughts that CBT works with. Five thinking patterns to catch first is a good place to start.
